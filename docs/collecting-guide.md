# GRAIUL — collecting folklore ethically

**English** · [Română](collecting-guide.ro.md)

Proverbs, riddles, charms and carols belong to the people who keep them alive. This guide is for anyone who writes down what a person tells them and wants to add it to GRAIUL. It is short because the rule is simple: **ask, say what will happen, write down the answer, and respect it.**

To put what you collected into the archive, follow `contributor-guide.md`.

## 1. Before you ask

- Say who you are and what GRAIUL is: an open archive, on the web, that anyone can read and copy.
- Say what will be published: the text, the region and the county, the source line (“told by …”) and, if the person agrees, their name.
- Say under which licence: the code is MIT; an entry can state `public-domain`, `CC0-1.0` or `CC-BY-SA-4.0`; if it states nothing, it is under the same licence as the repository. Ask the person whether they accept it.
- Say that they can say no, can stop, can ask you to leave something out, and can ask to stay unnamed, now or later.

## 2. Writing it down

- Write the text exactly as it is said, with the local words and pronunciation you can render, not “corrected”. If a word is unclear, say so in the note instead of guessing.
- Note the **region** and, if you know it, the **county** where the person learned it, not where they live now.
- Choose the **consent** value: `informed` when the person agreed and may be named; `anonymised` when they agreed and asked not to be named. Put your own name or initials in `collector`.
- Do not put in the entry the person’s address, phone number, health or anything else about them: the source line is “told by a woman from village X, born in the 1940s” or the name they gave you, nothing more.

## 3. Charms (descântece)

- Many charms are passed on only to certain people, at certain times, or never aloud to strangers. If the person tells you not to publish, **do not publish**, even if you could.
- A charm always needs a consent and a `purpose` (what it is said for, in a few words). The page shows it as heritage with a note; never write that it cures or protects, and never offer it as advice.
- If the charm names an illness, write the name as the person said it; do not add medical claims.

## 4. Published sources

- A text taken from a book or a web page: give the title, the author and the page, or the address (`source_url`, starting with `https://`), and set `consent: published`.
- Copy only what the licence allows: public-domain works, or texts that state a free licence. A few single proverbs with their source are fine; a whole modern collection is not.
- When a text comes with a free licence (as the English equivalents from Wikiquote, CC BY-SA 4.0), state it in `license`, so that the people who reuse the data know the condition.

## 5. After it is published

- If the person or their family later asks you to remove or change an entry, open an issue with the entry id or write to the maintainers: it will be changed or removed.
- Show them the page when you can. Folklore that comes back to its bearers is the best review.
- Be kind to those who disagree about what a text “really” says: different villages, different versions. Add the other version as another entry, with its own region and source.

Thank you for collecting with care.
